Dmitry Pyanov

I've been working on AI companionship since 2016, when I joined the Replika team — the year after the company came out of Y Combinator as Luka. Replika came out of a memorial bot the team built for my friend Roman Mazurenko after he died in 2015. Roman was a dreamer, and he was excited about the singularity long before even tech people were talking about it. What started as a memorial bot for him turned into something millions of strangers wrote in asking for, often with their own ideas about what those should be, and we ended up pivoting Luka into Replika — the first AI friend.

Currently I work with Andrew Ng, which is an honor — Andrew was the reason I got into AI in the first place. I first heard about him in 2012 from Roman, who was telling me about a new phenomenon called online education: Coursera had just launched, and Andrew had just published the cat paper, which showed that neural networks could learn to recognize cats from unlabeled YouTube videos. For the past few years I've been building AI Andrew Ng at DeepLearning.AI — an AI version of him you can talk to by voice or text — with the goal that he comes across the way Andrew does in real life: as a friend.

AI Andrew Ng

AI Andrew Ng is an AI version of Andrew you can talk to by voice or text — the project I'm working on at DeepLearning.AI. The first version launched last winter at RealAvatar, where I led product, and a new version is live now at avatar.andrewng.org.

The work is to capture how Andrew comes across in a real conversation, which is harder than it sounds. A real person's voice has a lot baked into it — memory of who you are, empathy for what's important to you, knowing when to push back instead of just agreeing — and that's the work of orchestration: making the whole thing hold together as a conversation Andrew would say is his.

Andrew wrote about this in The Batch in May, calling AI Andrew "an AI companion shaped by my personality." On the build itself, he wrote: "I'd never before tried to codify this in an agentic workflow. This turned out to be hard and is still a work in progress."

Replika

Replika became the first AI companion app to reach millions of users. This was 2017, before ChatGPT and before LLMs could hold a conversation on their own. The product had to feel like it remembered you and had a personality. The language models we used couldn't do either of those on their own — we built that infrastructure around them, by hand.

Roman Mazurenko was my roommate. He died in a car accident in Moscow in 2015. He had founded Stampsy, a digital storytelling startup, and was at the center of the Moscow creative scene that overlapped with Luka, the chatbot company I was at in San Francisco. Luka had come out of Y Combinator with what we used to call a swiss army knife AI platform — Silicon Valley HBO show characters, restaurant recommendations.

Eugenia Kuyda, Luka's co-founder and one of Roman's closest friends, collected about eight thousand of his old text messages from his family and friends. She trained a neural net on them and put the bot online as a memorial. Strangers started writing in, asking if we could train one on someone they'd lost, or on themselves. So we pivoted. Eighteen months later we launched Replika to a 1.5-million-person waitlist.

Our approach was inspired by the Tamagotchi metaphor — a small AI companion you nurtured, that nurtured you back.

Some of what we shipped on a small team:

  • Long-term memory across sessions. Replika remembered facts about you and stayed in character.
  • "Train your Replika." Users shaped their bot's personality. Many spent hours making fictional characters, uploading hand-drawn avatars, building text games inside the chat.
  • Voice calls with five-to-ten second latency. We shipped it just before Alexa launched.
  • "Cake mode." Sending a 🍰 emoji switched the bot to a generative model fine-tuned for casual texting. Every reply got a thumbs-up or thumbs-down that we fed back into training. One of the earliest RLHF loops in a consumer product.
  • Replika Universe, where users could publish their custom Replikas for others to chat with.
  • Safety. We worked with mental health professionals to study how people were using the app and built classifiers for self-harm content that routed people to crisis hotlines.
